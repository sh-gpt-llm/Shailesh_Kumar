Every enterprise architecture leader eventually hits the same wall: too many emerging technology bets,
not enough structured way to decide which ones deserve real investment. Over the last two years leading
the Emerging Technology & Innovation function at a global life sciences enterprise, I built and refined a
framework I call **TIE** — Triage, Invest, Expand.

## The Problem

Generative AI and Agentic AI use cases arrive faster than governance models can absorb them. Without a
structured triage mechanism, organizations either say "no" to everything (killing innovation velocity) or
"yes" to everything (creating shadow IT, redundant tooling, and unmanaged risk exposure).

## The Three Stages

### 1. Triage
Every incoming idea — from a business unit, a vendor pitch, or an internal hackathon — is scored against
four dimensions: **business value potential**, **technical feasibility**, **regulatory/compliance risk**,
and **strategic alignment** to the enterprise technology radar. This produces a fast, defensible go/no-go/park
decision within days, not months.

### 2. Invest
Ideas that clear triage move into a bounded, time-boxed pilot with clear success criteria, a named business
sponsor, and Responsible AI guardrails baked in from day one — not retrofitted after the fact. This is where
most organizations lose discipline; TIE keeps investment capped until value is proven.

### 3. Expand
Proven pilots graduate into the enterprise architecture — with production-grade security review, scalability
validation, and integration into the standard technology stack. This is the industrialization step that turns
a promising prototype into a governed enterprise capability.

## Why It Works

TIE gives architecture, innovation, and finance teams a shared vocabulary. It replaces ad-hoc "AI committee"
debates with a repeatable, auditable process — which is exactly what's needed when you're trying to move fast
on GenAI while operating inside a regulated, risk-aware enterprise.
